Urbanization is an ever-growing concern in behavioural ecology. Crucially, urbanization is accelerating at the global level to accommodate the increasing human population. From enlarging existing cities to creating new ones altogether, huge swathes of natural landscapes are being converted to facilitate humans occupation. This triggers a number of changes in the environment, including an increase in impermeable surfaces and human infrastructure. This, in turn, can have great impacts on the survival and behaviour of species, and rising global urbanization is associated with a decrease in biodiversity.

While many species are negatively affected by urbanization, some speceis can tolerate or even benefit from living in urban areas. Species like the American crow have adapted to living in urban areas and their abundance has historically increased with increasing urbanization. How these species behaviouraly adapt to urban living is a particularly growing field of research and many studies have shown how flexible and adaptable these species can be. Behavioural adaptations can include changes in foraging behaviour, such as preference for anthropogenic foods or foraging on sources of human refuse such as garbages and dumpters. Adaptations of vigilance behaviours are also recorded, such as increased tolerance to human proximity and vehicular disturbances.

One glaring issue in many studies on the behaviours of urban species are general comparisons between "urban" and "non-urban" populations, with oftentimes few considerations given to the spectrum of the effects of urbanization affecting these two populations. More importantly, urban areas themselves are not homogeneous, and cities have many different micro-environments of varying levels of urbanization. The many parks and green spaces in St. Catharines, Ontario, differ greatly from the concrete jungles that are its commercial areas. These two examples, commercial and green areas, stand on two ends of the urbanization spectrum, and demonstrate sharp differences between areas within a city. 

Green areas are typically quieter and greener areas in a city, where the increased presence of trees and wider patches of grass and vegetation offers urban species a comparatively more natural area to forage in. These spaces will have longer lines of sight, fewer and different types of disturbances, as well as a greater presence of more natural foods such as seeds and invertebrates. On the other hand, commercial areas are more disturbed and will have a greater presence of vehicles than in green spaces. Louder ambient noise levels, more impermeable surfaces and shorter lines of sight also characterize these environments. Finally, commercial areas can have an increased presence of human food sources such as litter and garbage and will harbor fewer natural food sources to forage on.
